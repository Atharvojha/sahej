Two steps done. So far, so good.

04 / The lab is across town. She puts it off.

No test. No return visit. And the clinic never finds out.

05 / Same patient. Now with Sahej.

07 / The test gets done.

08 / Back at the clinic, on time.

03 / OUR APPROACH · FROM ACCESS TO COMPLETION

A little support.
A more complete loop.

01 / KEEP THE PLAN VISIBLE

CareLoop

The checklist of every step a doctor prescribes, closed one by one. A clinic sees which patients finished their medicine, tests and follow-ups, and which did not.

A familiar voice.
02 / NUDGE THE NEXT STEP

BoliCare

A voice-first nudge in the patient's own language. A call before the refill and the test are due, in Telugu, Hindi and English. A person can help when timing or travel is in the way.

Close, even from far away.
03 / BRING FAMILY INTO THE LOOP

FamilyBridge

A caregiver view, only with the patient's consent. Adult children living elsewhere can see what was missed and support the next step.

Working hypothesis

Next steps completed on time. Follow-up attendance, 90-day retention, cost per coordinated patient and clinics willing to pay are the measures that matter.

08 / SWOT ANALYSISExplore the five forces ↗

A real problem.
An unproven advantage.

What could work in Sahej’s favour, what still needs proof, and what the market could make difficult.

Concept assessment Strengths are potential design choices. The report has not established product-market fit, proprietary assets, retention or payer contracts.

What could helpWhat could hold it backInternal / the concept
S

Strengths

Potential concept advantages
  • One plan across two conditions

    Recurring care tasks span diabetes and hypertension, rather than a single disease-specific programme.

  • A potentially lighter service

    An organiser could cost less to deliver than clinician, coaching or CGM bundles. Unit economics are unproven.

  • More ways to participate

    Local language, voice and consented caregiver support could improve usability if patients prefer them.

  • The core job does not require AI

    Reliable scheduling and clear follow-through may reduce complexity and exposure to clinical risk.

W

Weaknesses

Internal evidence gaps
  • Demand is not validated

    No established Sahej evidence for users, retention, outcomes, acquisition, willingness to pay or payer contracts.

  • The features are easy to find elsewhere

    Reminders, records, appointments and condition tracking already exist in other offerings.

  • Another app can become another task

    Entering prescriptions and maintaining dates adds effort. Patients may abandon it if existing habits work better.

  • A reminder cannot remove every barrier

    Two WhatsApp reminders did not significantly improve attendance in one Punjab trial. Cost, access and motivation may matter more.

External / the market
O

Opportunities

External possibilities to test
  • Find the group with unfinished care tasks

    Disease burden and care gaps justify looking for patients with recurring coordination problems. Prevalence alone is not demand.

  • Meet patients in familiar channels

    Indic-language and voice usage make accessible communication worth testing, rather than assuming an English app suits everyone.

  • Look beyond the patient as payer

    Family, employer, insurer or health-system funding could improve affordability if value is measurable. India-specific willingness to pay is unverified.

  • Build alongside ABHA / ABDM

    Public personal health records could complement follow-through. They also weaken paid record storage as a standalone advantage.

T

Threats

External pressures
  • Free substitutes may be good enough

    Alarms, paper, pillboxes, family, WhatsApp and bundled apps can make a separate subscription unnecessary.

  • Incumbents can bundle the same features

    Practo, Apollo Sugar, specialist programmes and public record tools already overlap with the proposed service.

  • A low price does not prove conversion

    ₹100 a month is a scenario. Low switching costs and uncertain willingness to pay can weaken conversion and retention.

  • Trust can be lost in one wrong step

    Incorrect schedules, unwanted caregiver access, data misuse or inappropriate health interpretation can damage trust. Clinical advice adds safety and compliance demands.

The opportunity has to be earned.

The strongest opportunity is a specific group whose recurring care tasks remain fragmented after using today’s tools. The greatest risk is that free substitutes already do enough.

09 / PORTER’S FIVE FORCESINDIA · QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS

Five pressures.
One idea to prove.

A large health problem can still be a difficult business. Explore the forces around patient-facing chronic-care support.

Analyst judgment Pressure labels reflect the report’s qualitative assessment. Their positions and arrows are illustrative, not measured scores.

Choose a force to look closer ↗

01 / FIVE FORCES

Competitive rivalry

High

Many services compete for the same patient attention.

Specialist diabetes programmes, horizontal health apps, pharmacies and public record tools overlap on reminders, records and chronic-care support, even when their products differ.

THE ALTERNATIVE SET / DEPENDENCIES

Sugar.fit · BeatO · Fitterfly · Twin Health · Apollo Sugar · Practo · Eka Care · Healthify

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR SAHEJ

Reminders and records alone do not create a distinctive position. Sahej needs evidence that it completes a specific care task better than existing options.

02 / FIVE FORCES

Threat of new entrants

High for basic tools; moderate for integrated care

A task list is easy to copy. A trusted service is harder to build.

Basic reminder tools have low feature-level entry barriers. Integrated care requires trusted handling of health information, reliable workflows, meaningful integrations, clinical safety, distribution and evidence of improved task completion.

THE ALTERNATIVE SET / DEPENDENCIES

Low barriers: reminder apps and task lists. Higher barriers: trusted workflows and integrated care.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR SAHEJ

Those harder-to-build capabilities could create defensibility, but Sahej has not yet demonstrated them.

03 / FIVE FORCES

Bargaining power of buyers

High

Patients can decline a subscription and keep their clinical care.

Switching is easy and free alternatives are available. Direct willingness to pay for Sahej is unknown. Employers, insurers or providers may also require outcomes evidence, low per-member cost, data safeguards and flexible procurement.

THE ALTERNATIVE SET / DEPENDENCIES

MyTherapy · phone alarms · Practo · ABHA · institutional sponsors

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR SAHEJ

Keep the user and payer distinct. Patient value, sponsor economics and renewal value each need validation.

04 / FIVE FORCES

Bargaining power of suppliers

Moderate

Technology vendors are replaceable. Access channels may be less so.

Cloud and scheduling services have multiple vendors. Messaging policies and costs, app distribution, identity and record integrations can constrain reach. Provider or payer partners may control access to users and clinical workflow data.

THE ALTERNATIVE SET / DEPENDENCIES

Messaging channels · app stores · cloud vendors · record integrations · provider and payer partners

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR SAHEJ

Dependency rises if Sahej relies on one messaging platform or a proprietary integration. Preserve alternative routes to patients.

05 / FIVE FORCES

Threat of substitutes

Very high

The strongest alternative may already be part of everyday life.

Memory, notebooks, alarms, pillboxes, family, WhatsApp, pharmacy and appointment apps, clinicians and public records are low-cost substitutes embedded in routines. One WhatsApp reminder trial did not significantly improve follow-up attendance; the barrier may be more than forgetting.

THE ALTERNATIVE SET / DEPENDENCIES

Paper · alarms · pillboxes · family help · WhatsApp · existing apps · clinicians

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR SAHEJ

Prove value against those routines through task completion, continued use, trust and price acceptance. A feature checklist is not enough.

INDUSTRY ATTRACTIVENESS / THE REPORT’S VERDICT

A narrower job has
a stronger case.

Low

Generic reminders + records

Sold directly to patients: high rivalry, powerful substitutes, high buyer power and low switching costs.

11 / THE PRODUCT OPPORTUNITYIndia, beyond a single city

Worth pursuing
across India?

Yes—as a product hypothesis worth validating.

The research points to a meaningful chronic-care follow-up problem and a clinic-first opportunity in India. The need is compelling. A viable product still has to prove three things. Research judgment

01 / WILLINGNESS TO PAY

Will clinics pay about ₹100 per patient a month?

Test willingness to pay with independent clinics in different cities. Ask for a signed fee commitment, not a compliment.

02 / FOLLOW-UP COMPLETION

Do reminders and calls raise follow-up completion?

Compare enrolled patients with similar patients who were not contacted. The product has to improve completion, not just send more reminders.

03 / SUSTAINABLE OPERATIONS

Can coordination stay affordable as the product grows?

Track minutes per patient from the first week. Test whether coordinator, call and onboarding costs can stay below the price across different settings.

A business model to validate.Revisit the evidence ↗
